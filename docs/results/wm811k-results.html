Wafer defect classification · label-budget sweep

WM-811K sweep results

I trained four methods at five label budgets with three seeds each (60 runs) and scored every run on the same lot-disjoint test set of 34,601 wafers. The question was how much self-supervised and semi-supervised learning buys you when labels are scarce.

Which method to use

Gain is the paired difference against the supervised CNN, averaged over 3 seeds, in macro-F1 points. "Clear" means the best method leads the runner-up by more than 1.5 points on average and in all 3 seeds.

SimCLR leads below 10% of labels; FixMatch leads at 25%

SimCLR pretraining on all 739k maps is worth the most when labels are scarcest. At 1% (1,210 labeled wafers) it scores 0.66 against the CNN's 0.43. FixMatch overtakes it at 25% and stays level with the CNN at full labels. GBM on hand-made features beats the CNN at small budgets but stops improving at about 0.75.

Bands show ±1 standard deviation over 3 seeds. The x-axis is logarithmic. GBM has zero spread at 100% because every seed sees the same data and the trees are deterministic.

The gains hold seed by seed

Each seed draws one labeled subset, which every method trains on, so I can compare methods within a seed. Each mark shows the mean gain over the CNN, with a bar for the range across the 3 seeds. SimCLR's gain shrinks steadily from +24 points to slightly negative. FixMatch's gain stays above zero in every seed up to 25%, then disappears.

What the gains are worth in labels

Reading each score off the CNN's curve gives the labels the CNN would need to match it. SimCLR at 1% matches a CNN trained on about 6.7% of the labels. FixMatch at 25% matches a CNN on about 54%.

Interpolated linearly in log(label share) between the measured CNN budgets. A dash means the score is above anything the CNN reached.

Scratch is the class that separates the methods

Macro-F1 weights all nine classes equally, so a rare class nobody can find drags every method down. Scratch is the lowest-recall class at every budget for every method. With 8 labeled scratches at 1%, all four methods find essentially none. Below full labels, the CNN finds at most a quarter of them, while FixMatch reaches 58% at 25% labels. Loc and Edge-Loc are where SimCLR's low-budget lead comes from: at 1% the CNN finds 1–3% of them and SimCLR finds 42–52%.

01 recall

Each thumbnail is a test wafer of that class (dark dies are defective). The count under each class is how many labeled training wafers that budget gives you. GBM never gets Scratch recall above 0.08: its summary features cannot see a thin line of dies. SimCLR's Donut recall stays at 0.63–0.77 while the CNN's climbs to 0.89. That gap is consistent across budgets, but it rests on only 111 test wafers.

The exported model: where its 1,068 errors go

export picked FixMatch seed 1, the full-label run with the best validation macro-F1 (0.866). I re-scored it locally on the full test set: macro-F1 0.843, matching the 0.843 logged in Colab. Almost all of its mistakes involve the none class: 475 clean wafers flagged as defective and 289 defective wafers called clean.

Edge-Loc has the weakest precision (0.69): 275 of its 1,255 predictions are clean wafers. Loc and Edge-Loc are confused with each other 87 times. MLflow only logged recall for the sweep runs, so this is the only run with precision figures.

Sending low-confidence wafers to an engineer

The model's confidence matches its accuracy closely (expected calibration error 0.008), so a confidence threshold is a reliable way to route wafers. Sending every wafer under 0.9 confidence to a human means reviewing 6.6% of wafers, and it cuts automatic errors by three quarters (1,068 to 263). Macro-F1 on the wafers handled automatically rises from 0.843 to 0.941.

The wafers sent for review are mostly defective ones: at the 0.9 threshold, 19% of defective wafers go to a human but only 4% of clean ones do. That's probably the split you want, since defective wafers are the ones worth an engineer's time.

SimCLR pretraining stopped improving early

Contrastive loss fell from 2.96 to 2.34 in roughly the first 2,000 of 20,000 steps and ended at 2.28. Pretraining took 36 minutes. A flat loss doesn't prove the features stopped improving, so test a 5,000-step encoder before cutting.

  • Three seeds per point. Differences under about 2 points (SimCLR vs. FixMatch at 25%, anything at 100%) agree in direction across seeds, but three seeds can't establish that they're real.
  • Near-full has 30 test wafers, so one wafer moves its recall by 3.3 points. At 1% there is a single labeled Near-full example. Don't read anything into that class's swings.
  • Runtime: FixMatch took 9.7 minutes per run (2.4 hours for 15 runs), slower than the 5–8 minutes I estimated. The supervised CNN and SimCLR fine-tuning took 2.8 minutes each, GBM 0.3 minutes.

Next steps

  • Collect more Scratch labels. It is the biggest drag on macro-F1, and every neural method improves sharply on it as labels grow (CNN: 0.24 at 25% to 0.71 at 100%). A few hundred targeted scratch labels probably beat any method change.
  • Log precision and a confusion matrix per run. The sweep logs recall only, so the false alarms on clean wafers are invisible in MLflow.
  • Run 5 seeds at 10%, 25% and 100% to settle the close calls, especially SimCLR vs. FixMatch.
  • Try FixMatch on top of the SimCLR encoder. The two win at different budgets, and they combine naturally.
  • Set the deployment gate. Put gate.min_macro_f1 just under the sample macro-F1 that export printed, then commit models/best_model/.
